The data

What we started with

Collisions
Traffic collisions recorded by the San Diego Police Department on city streets, 2018 to 2025: 67,970 collisions and 139,192 people involved. Freeways patrolled by the CHP are not included. 2016 and 2017 are left out because their records are incomplete.
Injury level
Each person has an injury level. We group severe, serious and fatal injuries as serious or fatal. About 72% of people have no injury level recorded, which most likely means they were not hurt, so injury rates describe the people who were hurt.
Outside data
To describe each police beat we added the US Census American Community Survey (2020 to 2024), SANDAG roads, bikeways and transit stops, City of San Diego traffic counts, parking meters, zoning and police beat boundaries, and SDPD crime records (NIBRS, 2020 to 2025).

Limits

  • Each collision is counted once, even when several people are involved. Each person’s injury is counted separately.
  • Severity is a rate (serious or fatal injuries divided by people hurt), so busy and quiet areas can be compared fairly.
  • The records have no age or sex, which both matter a lot for how badly people are hurt.

Chapter 1

Where and when: the beat map

Measures
Frequency counts unique collision reports per police beat and year. The serious and fatal rate divides people with serious, severe or fatal injuries by people with a known injury level, shown only for beat years with at least 30 known injury records.
Classes
Classes are relative fifths of all 2018 to 2025 beat years (zero is its own class for the sparse fatal measures). The breaks are shared across years, so a color means the same thing every year.
Boundaries
The map uses the 125 San Diego Police Department beats. The city boundary file also holds county sheriff beats that reuse the same numbers; those are left out.

Limits

  • The map shows observed patterns, not risk adjusted for traffic or population.
  • Beats with no record in a year are grey.

Tableau dashboards

Dashboards
Three interactive dashboards built by Kyle VanDillen in Tableau Public from the same SDPD collision records: when fatal collisions happen (chapter 1), fatal and nonfatal collisions by road user, and the violations police cited as the cause (both chapter 2).
Numbers
The figures in each chapter’s summary box come from our own analysis of the 2018 to 2025 records of people with a known injury level, so small differences from the dashboards are possible.

Limits

  • The cause is the violation the officer cited, not an independent finding of what caused the crash.

Chapter 3

Correlation

Question
Do police beats with certain characteristics have a higher share of serious or fatal injuries?
Unit
One row per police beat. We used the 112 of 125 beats with at least 50 people with a known injury level, so each beat’s rate is stable.
Outcome
The beat’s serious and fatal injury rate, 2018 to 2025.
Inputs
44 characteristics in six groups: who is involved, when and why crashes happen, the streets, land use, residents, and crime. 17 come from our collision data (one of them also uses SANDAG street lengths) and 27 from the outside sources above.
Method
Spearman rank correlation. It ranks the beats from lowest to highest on each variable and checks whether the rankings line up, so a few extreme beats cannot distort the result. A link counts as clear when p is below .05: less than a 5% chance of a link that size appearing by luck.

Limits

  • These are patterns between places, not between people. A beat with many pedestrians having lower severity does not mean pedestrians are safer.
  • A correlation does not show cause and effect.
  • Many inputs move together (density, transit stops, intersections, beat area), so they are largely one signal, not separate findings.

Chapter 4

Regression

Question
When someone is hurt in a crash, what raises the chance that the injury is serious or fatal, once all the other factors are held equal?
Unit
One row per person hurt in a collision, 2018 to 2025: 38,230 people, of whom 2,033 (5.3%) were seriously hurt or killed.
Outcome
Yes or no: was this person seriously hurt or killed?
Inputs
11 factors chosen from the project question and road safety research: road user type (compared with drivers), truck or bus involved, night (8pm to 6am), weekend, unsafe speed, DUI, hit and run, the year 2020 (COVID), and the beat’s population density, average speed limit and median income.
Method
Logistic regression, the standard tool when the outcome is yes or no. Results are odds ratios: 1 means no effect, 2 means double the odds, 0.5 means half. Errors are grouped by police beat, because people in the same beat share the same beat inputs.
Checks
The model ranks who gets seriously hurt fairly well (AUC 0.78, where 0.5 is a coin flip and 1 is perfect). No two inputs overlap too much (every variance inflation factor is below 1.5). Running it again with only fatal injuries tells the same story.

Limits

  • Hit and run looks protective, but 88% of these cases carry the property damage hit and run code, so they are mostly low harm crashes by how they were recorded.
  • “Unsafe speed” (Vehicle Code 22350) means too fast for conditions. Officers often use it for rear end crashes in slow traffic, so it is not the same as high speed.
  • The results show association, not proof of cause.
