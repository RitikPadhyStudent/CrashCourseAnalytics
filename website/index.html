San Diego traffic collisions, 2018 to 2025

Who pays
the highest price
on San Diego streets?

We studied 67,970 collisions on San Diego city streets to find out who is most likely to be seriously hurt or killed, and when and where it happens.

The question

Most crashes on city streets end with a dented car and a minor injury. A few end with someone in hospital or dead. We wanted to know what separates the two, so we compared collisions by four things.

  • Who

    Drivers, passengers, pedestrians, bicyclists and motorcyclists.

  • When

    Year, day of the week and hour of the day, with 2020 read separately because of COVID.

  • Where

    The 125 San Diego police beats, and what their streets and residents are like.

  • How

    What the police said caused the crash: speeding, DUI, hit and run, red lights, unsafe turns.

Most crashes end with a dent and a sore neck. The ones that end in a hospital bed fall on the people with no car around them.

The short version

No time for the whole story? Here is where it ends up.

    Fewer crashes, not fewer serious injuries

    Collisions peaked at 10,174 in 2019 and fell about 25% by 2025. Serious and fatal injuries fell only about 5%.

Questions people ask

The short answers. Methods has the details.

Where does the data come from?

Traffic collision records from the San Diego Police Department, 2018 to 2025. Chapter 3 also uses the US Census, SANDAG road and transit data, City of San Diego data and SDPD crime records to describe each police beat.

Why does the study start in 2018?

The 2016 and 2017 records are incomplete. 2017 has only a few hundred records, so including them would make the trends misleading.

What counts as a serious injury?

An injury the officer recorded as severe, serious or fatal. Minor, visible, possible and pain only injuries count as not serious.

Why do so many people have no injury level?

About 72% of people in the records have none. There is no code for “not hurt”, so a blank most likely means the person was not injured. Injury rates on this site describe the people who were hurt.

Are freeways included?

No. Freeways are patrolled by the California Highway Patrol, so this study covers city streets only.

Does this prove what causes severe crashes?

No. The analysis finds patterns and holds other factors equal where it can, but it cannot prove cause. The records also have no age or sex, which both matter a lot.

Why are road users compared with drivers?

Drivers are the largest group and the people best protected by a car, so they are the natural baseline for everyone else.

Can I see the code and the data?

Yes. Everything is in the project’s GitHub repository, including the data notes that explain known problems in the records.
