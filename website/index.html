San Diego · Traffic collisions · 2018–2025

Who pays the highest price on San Diego streets?

We studied every traffic collision the San Diego Police Department recorded on city streets from 2018 to 2025, to find out who is most likely to be seriously hurt or killed, and when and where it happens. Pedestrians, bicyclists and motorcyclists get particular attention, because they have no car around them.

City streets only · freeways patrolled by CHP are not included

The data at a glance

  • 67,970collisions on city streets, 2018–2025
  • 139,192people involved in those collisions
  • 2,036people seriously hurt or killed
  • 427people killed
  • 125police beats compared

The question

Most crashes on city streets end with a dented car and a minor injury. A few end with someone in hospital or dead. We wanted to know what separates the two. So we ask whether the number of collisions, and how severe the injuries are, differ by four things:

  • WhoDrivers, passengers, pedestrians, bicyclists and motorcyclists.
  • WhenYear, day of the week and hour of the day, with 2020 (COVID) read separately.
  • WhereThe 125 San Diego police beats, and what their streets and residents are like.
  • HowWhat caused the crash: speeding, DUI, hit-and-run, red lights, unsafe turns.

The short version

No time for the whole story? Here is where it ends up.

  1. Fewer crashes, but not fewer serious injuries. Collisions peaked at 10,174 in 2019 and fell about 25% by 2025. Serious and fatal injuries fell only about 5%. See the beat map →
  2. The busiest places are not the most dangerous. Dense central beats have the most crashes, but a lower share of severe ones. Spread-out beats with faster driving have a higher share of people seriously hurt. See the correlation →
  3. People outside a car pay the highest price. In otherwise similar crashes, pedestrians have about 9 times, motorcyclists about 9 times, and bicyclists about 5 times the odds of a driver of being seriously hurt or killed. See the regression →
  4. Night and drunk driving make crashes worse. Crashes between 8pm and 6am roughly double the odds of a serious injury, and DUI crashes raise them about 2.5 times. See the regression →

How we did it

  • Count each crash once. A collision with several people in it is one collision, but each person's injury counts.
  • Measure severity as a rate. We divide serious and fatal injuries by the number of people hurt, so busy and quiet areas can be compared fairly.
  • Look at patterns first. Trends over time, differences by road user, hour, day, police beat and cause of crash.
  • Then test them together. A regression checks which factors still matter once the others are held constant.

Data sources

  • US Census American Community Survey, 2020–2024
  • SANDAG roads, bikeways and transit stops
